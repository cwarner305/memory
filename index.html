EXPERT JOURNEY 01

Encoding

Driving question: What makes information more likely to be learned?

REVIEW · 3.1–3.4

Reconnect with the vocabulary

Say a definition and an example before opening each term.

Encoding

Transforming incoming information into a form that can be stored.

Example: Connecting a new vocabulary word to a familiar example.

Selective attention

Focusing on some information while filtering other input.

Example: Following the color sequence while ignoring conversations.

Automatic processing

Processing some information with little deliberate effort.

Example: A practiced reader rapidly recognizes a familiar word.

Effortful processing

Processing that requires deliberate attention and effort.

Example: Studying the difference between two unfamiliar psychology terms.

Shallow processing

Encoding surface features rather than meaning.

Example: Noticing whether a word is capitalized.

Deep processing

Encoding by thinking about meaning and connections.

Example: Explaining why a scenario demonstrates encoding.

Semantic encoding

Encoding information according to its meaning.

Example: Connecting the word apple to the category fruit.

Schema

A mental framework that organizes knowledge and expectations.

Example: Familiar words in the grids were easier to organize than unfamiliar symbols.

Elaborative rehearsal

Connecting new information to meaning or existing knowledge.

Example: Linking working memory to mentally calculating a tip.

NEW · 3.5–3.6

Extend your understanding

Say a definition and an example before opening each term.

Encoding failure

Information was not adequately encoded in the first place.

Example: You looked at a page while distracted and never learned the definition.

Keep these ideas separate

Automatic does not mean every easy task was learned automatically. A skill may become easier through practice. Deep processing focuses on meaning; simply repeating a word is maintenance rehearsal.

Check your vocabulary

Choose an answer, read the feedback, and correct any mistakes. You can retry each question.

Prepare your expert teaching card

Explain encoding, contrast shallow and deep processing, and show how a schema can help organize information. Explain how encoding failure differs from losing an established memory.

EXPERT JOURNEY 02

Storage

Driving question: What is being held, and what are you doing with it?

REVIEW · 3.1–3.4

Reconnect with the vocabulary

Say a definition and an example before opening each term.

Storage

Retaining encoded information over time.

Example: Keeping yesterday’s lesson available for today.

Sensory memory

Very brief retention of incoming sensory information.

Example: A fleeting impression of an image after it disappears.

Iconic memory

Very brief visual sensory memory.

Example: A momentary visual trace of a flashed grid.

Echoic memory

Brief auditory sensory memory lasting a few seconds.

Example: A short-lived trace of words you just heard.

Short-term memory

Briefly holding a limited amount of information.

Example: Holding a number long enough to enter it.

Working memory

A limited system for temporarily holding and manipulating information.

Example: Holding a Simon sequence while updating the next response.

Long-term memory

Relatively lasting retention of knowledge, skills, and experiences.

Example: Remembering how to ride a bicycle or defining a familiar word.

Chunking

Grouping separate pieces into meaningful units.

Example: Reading 1 7 7 6 as the familiar year 1776.

Maintenance rehearsal

Repeating information to keep it active.

Example: Repeating a short sequence until it is your turn.

Cognitive load

The demands a task places on limited working-memory resources.

Example: Tracking a sequence while also doing mental arithmetic.

Primacy effect

Often better memory for items near the beginning of a list.

Example: Remembering the first few words from class.

Recency effect

Often better memory for items near the end of a list.

Example: Remembering the final words immediately after hearing them.

NEW · 3.5–3.6

Extend your understanding

Say a definition and an example before opening each term.

Decay theory

The proposal that memory traces weaken with time, especially without maintenance or use.

Example: A trace may weaken during a delay; time alone does not prove decay caused a recall failure.

Keep these ideas separate

Short-term memory emphasizes holding; working memory emphasizes holding AND working with information. Chunking makes meaningful units; it does not give everyone unlimited capacity. A simple three-box model is useful, but memory is not three literal brain containers.

Walk partners through sensory, short-term/working, and long-term memory. Contrast holding with manipulating. Demonstrate chunking and explain why forgetting after a delay does not prove decay.

EXPERT JOURNEY 03

Retrieval

Driving question: How do cues help—and sometimes mislead—remembering?

REVIEW · 3.1–3.4

Reconnect with the vocabulary

Say a definition and an example before opening each term.

Retrieval

Accessing stored information.

Example: Bringing yesterday’s vocabulary into mind.

Recall

Producing learned information with few or no external cues; recall can also be cued.

Example: Writing a definition without answer choices.

Recognition

Identifying previously encountered information when it appears again.

Example: Selecting a studied word from a list.

Schema

A framework of knowledge that can guide organization and remembering.

Example: A restaurant schema helps you reconstruct a visit, but may also lead you to assume a detail.

NEW · 3.5–3.6

Extend your understanding

Say a definition and an example before opening each term.

Retrieval cue

A hint or stimulus that helps access stored information.

Example: The category fruit helps bring pear to mind.

Priming

Prior exposure influences later processing, often without awareness of the connection.

Example: Seeing doctor can make nurse faster to recognize; this is not the same as deliberately recalling a list.

Context-dependent memory

Retrieval can improve when the external setting matches the learning setting.

Example: A room’s features may provide cues associated with learning.

State-dependent memory

Retrieval can be influenced by a match between internal states at learning and remembering.

Example: An internal-state match can serve as a cue; students should use safe study strategies rather than trying to change their state.

Schema activation

Bringing a relevant mental framework into use.

Example: Thinking of a grocery store helps organize and retrieve food words.

Retrieval failure

Difficulty accessing information that has been stored.

Example: A name returns after someone supplies its first sound.

Reconstructive memory

Remembering involves rebuilding information rather than replaying a perfect recording.

Example: Expectations and later information can shape a remembered event.

False memory

Remembering something inaccurately or remembering something that did not occur.

Example: Reporting a related word that never appeared.

Confabulation

Unintentionally producing fabricated or distorted memories, sometimes to fill gaps, without intending to deceive.

Example: A person sincerely reports an invented explanation for a memory gap; one wrong list word alone does not establish confabulation.

Keep these ideas separate

Recognition supplies the item to evaluate; recall asks you to produce it. A retrieval cue helps access a memory; priming can influence processing without conscious remembering. Confidence is not proof of accuracy.

NEW · 3.6

Make remembering more likely

Spaced practice: return to the material across several sessions. Retrieval practice: close your notes, try to produce an answer, then check and correct it.

Ebbinghaus forgetting curve: forgetting often drops rapidly at first and then slows under a given set of conditions. There is no single exact timetable for everyone. Review and successful retrieval can support retention.

Individual final challenge

Alex rereads definitions while checking messages. The words look familiar. Two days later, Alex cannot explain them without choices and mixes up proactive and retroactive interference.

  2. Explain how working-memory demands could affect studying.
  3. Distinguish recognizing a term from recalling its meaning.
  4. Create one proactive and one retroactive interference example. Label the target memory.
  5. Recommend a study plan using meaning, spaced practice, and retrieval practice. Explain why each step helps.

EXPERT JOURNEY 04

Forgetting

Driving question: Was it never learned, hard to access, or disrupted by other information?

REVIEW · 3.1–3.4

Reconnect with the vocabulary

Say a definition and an example before opening each term.

Encoding

Getting information into a storable form.

Example: Learning the new term rather than only looking at it.

Storage

Retaining information over time.

Example: Keeping a learned definition available.

Retrieval

Accessing stored information.

Example: Producing the definition when asked.

Recall and recognition

Recall produces information; recognition identifies information presented again.

Example: Explaining a term versus choosing it from options.

NEW · 3.5–3.6

Extend your understanding

Say a definition and an example before opening each term.

Encoding failure

Information was not adequately encoded.

Example: You were distracted when the name was introduced.

Retrieval failure

Stored information is temporarily difficult to access.

Example: The name returns with a helpful cue.

Decay theory

The proposal that memory traces weaken over time.

Example: Weakening traces are one proposed explanation, not proof that every forgotten item decayed.

Interference

Information competes with or disrupts other learning or remembering.

Example: Two similar passwords become confused.

Proactive interference

Older information interferes with learning or recalling newer information.

Example: Your old password keeps coming to mind when you need the new one. OLD → NEW.

Retroactive interference

Newer information interferes with recalling older information.

Example: After learning the new password, you struggle to recall the old one. NEW → OLD.

Ebbinghaus forgetting curve

A pattern of relatively rapid initial forgetting followed by a slower decline under particular learning and testing conditions.

Example: It describes a general pattern, not a universal percentage or schedule for every learner.

Spaced practice

Distributing learning across multiple sessions.

Example: Reviewing on several days rather than only the night before.

Retrieval practice

Trying to bring learned information to mind, then checking and correcting it.

Example: Closing the guide, defining terms, and checking the definitions.

Keep these ideas separate

Identify the TARGET memory first. If the target is NEW and OLD intrudes, it is proactive. If the target is OLD and NEW intrudes, it is retroactive. An incorrect answer does not reveal its cause by itself.
